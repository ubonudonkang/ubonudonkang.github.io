Business Analyst Interview Questions, Answered by a Practising BA

Most business analyst interviews follow a pattern: questions about what you have done, questions about documents and techniques, and scenarios that test how you think when people disagree. Here are the questions that come up most, grouped by type, with what the interviewer is checking and a short model answer for each.

01How BA interviews are structured

The format varies by employer, but most BA hiring processes combine some of these:

  • A screening call about your background and why you want the role
  • A competency interview on documents, techniques and tools
  • Scenario questions about stakeholders, changing requirements and testing
  • A practical task: write user stories for a feature, map a process, or critique a requirement
  • A panel with the people you'd work with, often including a developer or product owner

Across all of them, interviewers are checking the same three things: can you find the real problem, can you write it down so it can't be misread, and can you get people who disagree to a decision. The questions below are grouped the way interviews usually run: behavioural, technical, scenario and domain. Each has what to cover and a short model answer. Make the model answers your own; an interviewer can tell a recited answer from a lived one.

02Behavioural questions

Behavioural questions ask what you have done, not what you would do. Answer them with STAR: the Situation, your Task, the Actions you took, and the Result, with a number if you have one. Spend most of the time on the actions.

"Tell me about yourself."

How to answer: Two minutes, in three parts: where you are now, the one or two pieces of work that show you can do this job, and why this role is the next step. Leave out your full history; the CV has it.

Model answer: "I'm an operations officer moving into business analysis. For three years I've handled account-opening exceptions, so I know where that process breaks and why. Last year I documented the exception steps for my team and cut our rework noticeably. I've since mapped the process in BPMN and written a BRD and user stories for it as a portfolio project, which I can walk you through. This role appeals because it's the same kind of problem at a bigger scale."

"Why business analysis?" or "How does your background prepare you?"

How to answer: For career changers this is the most important question in the interview. Translate what you've done into BA language and name one concrete example. Banking operations staff know the processes BAs are hired to fix; customer service staff see where systems fail every day; researchers already frame questions and read data.

Model answer: "In customer service I spent most days working around the same three system problems. I started logging them with the steps to reproduce them, and two were fixed once IT could see exactly what was happening. That's the part of my job I enjoy most: finding the real problem and writing it down so someone can fix it. Business analysis is that, full time."

"Tell me about a process you improved."

How to answer: Pick one process, give its numbers before and after, and say what you personally did. Here's how I'd answer it, using work from Access Bank:

  • Situation: end-of-day GL reconciliation took analysts eight hours every day, which delayed overnight reporting.
  • Task: lead the requirements for automating it with RPA.
  • Actions: mapped the as-is process in BPMN, wrote the bot specification with its exception rules, and coordinated UAT with Finance, IT and Audit.
  • Result: reconciliation dropped to 45 minutes, an approximately 91% reduction. The case study has the detail.

"Tell me about a time you dealt with a difficult stakeholder."

How to answer: Show that you looked for the reason behind the behaviour, not that you won. Say what they needed, what you changed, and how the relationship ended up.

Model answer: "A head of operations kept rejecting our process maps late in review. When I met her one to one, it turned out her team's month-end workload had never been considered. I added her team to the workshops, mapped month-end as its own scenario, and she signed off the next version. She asked for me on the following project."

"Tell me about a mistake you made."

How to answer: Choose a real mistake with a real consequence, own it in one sentence, then spend the answer on what you did to fix it and what you do differently now.

Model answer: "I once wrote acceptance criteria that covered the normal flow but not a failed balance check. The gap showed up in UAT and cost us two days. I fixed the stories that week, and since then I write at least one unhappy path for every story and review them with a tester before refinement."

"How do you learn a new domain quickly?"

How to answer: Show a method: read the rules and documents that already exist, sit with the people who do the work, map the process, and check your understanding back with an expert.

Model answer: "I start with what's written down: policies, regulations, existing procedures. Then I sit with the people doing the work for a day and map what actually happens. I play the map back to a subject expert and ask them to correct it. Within a week or two I usually understand the process well enough to ask good questions."

03Technical questions

Technical questions check that you know the documents, techniques and tools of the job. Give a definition in one sentence, then an example; the example is what convinces.

"What does a business analyst do?"

How to answer: Go beyond "gathering requirements". Follow the work from problem to value.

Model answer: "A BA makes sure the right thing gets built. I work out the real business problem, agree with stakeholders what the solution must do, write it down so developers can build it and testers can test it, and check at the end that it delivers what was promised." What does a business analyst actually do? walks through a real week.

"Walk me through how you'd write a BRD."

How to answer: Start before the document: the problem, who signs off and how success is measured. Then name the main sections and finish with sign-off. Mentioning traceability to test cases sets you apart.

Model answer: "First I agree the problem, the approvers and the success measures. Then I write the background, objectives, scope with what's out, stakeholders, the as-is and to-be process, numbered and prioritised requirements, non-functional requirements, assumptions and sign-off. Every requirement gets an ID so it can be traced to stories and test cases." My BRD guide has the full structure.

"How do you write a good user story?"

How to answer: Give the format, then an example with acceptance criteria in Given/When/Then, including one unhappy path. Mention that stories should be small enough for a sprint and testable.

Model answer: "As a retail customer, I want to submit a treasury bill request online, so that I can invest without visiting a branch. Given my balance covers the amount and charges, when I submit, then a request is created with a reference. Given my balance is too low, when I submit, then I'm blocked and told why." See user stories and acceptance criteria for more.

"What's the difference between functional and non-functional requirements?"

How to answer: One sentence each, then one example of each.

Model answer: "Functional requirements say what the system does: the system must hold the investment amount on submission. Non-functional requirements say how well it does it: search results must load within three seconds for 95% of requests."

"Which elicitation techniques do you use, and when?"

How to answer: The point is matching the technique to the situation, so give the "when" for each.

Model answer: "Interviews for depth with one person, workshops when several teams must agree, observation when people can't describe what they do, document analysis for existing rules, and prototypes when users need to see something before they can react to it."

"How do you prioritise requirements?"

How to answer: Name a method, then say who decides. Prioritisation is a business decision; the BA facilitates and records it.

Model answer: "I usually use MoSCoW: must, should, could, won't this time. I run it as a session with the product owner and the key stakeholders, make the trade-offs visible, and record the outcome so it doesn't get reopened every week."

"What is a requirements traceability matrix, and why use one?"

How to answer: Define it, then say what it catches.

Model answer: "An RTM links each requirement to its source, the story that delivers it, the design and the test that proves it. It shows gaps: a requirement with no test, or a feature nobody asked for. I keep it current through UAT so sign-off is based on evidence." See the RTM guide.

"What is UAT, and what is the BA's role in it?"

How to answer: Define it, then list what the BA actually does in it.

Model answer: "User acceptance testing confirms the solution meets the business need, tested by business users. I plan it, write or review test cases from the requirements, support the testers, triage defects with the team, and collect sign-off."

"How comfortable are you with SQL and APIs?"

How to answer: Be honest. BAs aren't expected to build either, but say what you can do with them.

Model answer: "I can write a query with a join and a filter to check data during analysis and testing, and I can read an API's request and response to write integration requirements. I wouldn't design either, but I can work with the people who do."

"How is a BA different from a project manager or a product owner?"

How to answer: Say what each owns, then how you'd work with each, because on many teams the roles overlap.

Model answer: "The project manager owns the plan, budget and timeline. The product owner owns the product's priorities and backlog. I own understanding what the solution must do and making sure it meets the business need. In practice I work with the product owner on the backlog every day and with the project manager on scope and risk." BA vs product owner vs product manager goes deeper.

04Scenario questions

Scenario questions ask what you would do. Interviewers are listening for a sequence: understand, make the problem visible, involve the right people, decide, record. For the stakeholder scenarios, the 4C Framework gives you a structure to talk through: clarity on the problem, productive conflict, documented consensus, then committed owners. If you don't have work experience yet, use a portfolio project and say so.

"A stakeholder keeps changing the requirements. What do you do?"

How to answer: Find the need behind the changes, then use change control. They want to hear that you protect scope without being obstructive.

Model answer: "I'd ask what's driving the changes; often the real objective was never agreed. I'd confirm it with them, then put every further change through change control with its impact on time and cost, so the sponsor decides with the facts in front of them."

"Two senior stakeholders want opposite things."

How to answer: Surface the conflict early, make the trade-offs visible and get a documented decision from whoever owns it.

Model answer: "I'd meet each separately to understand what they need and why, then bring them together with the options and the consequences of each laid out. If they still disagree, the sponsor decides, and I record the decision and the reason in the decision log."

"Developers say a requirement can't be built as written."

How to answer: Go back to the business need; the requirement is one way of meeting it, not the only way.

Model answer: "I'd ask what makes it hard, then go back to the need behind the requirement. Usually there's another way to meet it. I'd take the options back to the business owner and update the requirement once they've chosen."

"UAT finds a serious defect a week before go-live."

How to answer: Assess impact, agree severity with the business, and escalate with options rather than alarm.

Model answer: "I'd confirm the defect, assess which processes and customers it affects, and agree its severity with the business owner. Then I'd take options to the sponsor: fix and retest, go live with a workaround, or move the date, with the risk of each."

"Nobody will sign off the requirements."

How to answer: Find out who is holding back and why, address the specific objections, and name the approvers at the start next time.

Model answer: "I'd ask each approver what's stopping them and list the objections. Most are specific and fixable. I'd resolve those, set a review date, and next time I'd agree the approvers and the sign-off date before writing a word."

05Domain questions for banking and fintech roles

For roles in Nigerian banks and fintechs, expect questions that test whether you understand the business you'll be analysing. You don't need to be an expert; showing you've thought about the controls and the failure points is enough.

"How does a transfer move between banks, and where can it fail?"

How to answer: Describe the flow in plain steps, then name the failure points, because that is where requirements come from.

Model answer: "The customer starts the transfer, the sending bank checks the balance and debits the account, the instruction goes through the interbank switch to the receiving bank, which credits the beneficiary and confirms. It can fail at the balance check, at the switch, or after the debit but before the credit, which is why reversals, reconciliation and clear customer messages matter."

"What is KYC, and how does it affect onboarding requirements?"

How to answer: Define it, then link it to requirements: what must be captured and verified, and what limits apply until it is.

Model answer: "Know your customer: verifying who a customer is before and while you serve them. In Nigeria that usually means identity checks such as BVN or NIN, and the CBN's tiered KYC means account limits depend on how much has been verified. So onboarding requirements must say what data is captured, how it's verified, and what limits apply at each tier."

"What is maker-checker, and why do regulated processes need it?"

How to answer: Define it with an example; mention the audit trail.

Model answer: "One person makes or reviews a transaction and a different person approves it, so no single person can complete a sensitive action alone. In requirements that becomes a rule like: the system shall not let the officer who reviewed a request also approve it, with both names recorded in the audit trail."

"What does reconciliation mean, and what causes breaks?"

How to answer: Define it, give causes, and say what a BA would specify.

Model answer: "Reconciliation checks that two records of the same money agree, such as the bank's ledger and a settlement report. Breaks come from timing differences, failed or duplicated postings, manual keying errors and reference mismatches. Requirements should cover matching rules, how exceptions are flagged and who resolves them."

"What do you know about open banking in Nigeria?"

How to answer: Show you know the regulatory basis and what it means for requirements, not just the buzzword.

Model answer: "The CBN issued a regulatory framework in 2021 and operational guidelines in 2023. Banks and fintechs share customer-permissioned data through APIs, with data grouped by risk and access depending on a participant's tier. For a BA the big requirements areas are consent, security and logging." My open banking guide explains the framework.

06Questions to ask the interviewer

Ask two or three. Good questions show you're already thinking about the work, and the answers tell you whether you want the job.

  • "What does a typical project look like here, from request to go-live?" Tells you how mature the delivery process is.
  • "Which documents do BAs own, and which templates do you use?" Tells you what you'll actually write.
  • "How do BAs work with product owners and developers day to day?" Tells you where the role's boundaries sit.
  • "Who signs off requirements, and how long does that usually take?" Tells you how decisions get made.
  • "What would a successful first 90 days look like in this role?" Tells you what they'll judge you on.
  • "What's the biggest challenge the team is facing right now?" Gives you something specific to address in your follow-up note.

07Your CV and portfolio

The interview starts with your CV, so make it carry the same evidence your answers will. Lead each role with results, use the words from the job advert for tools and methods you've really used, and keep it in a single column so applicant tracking systems can read it. My free business analyst CV template is built that way, with a short guide to tailoring it.

Bring a short portfolio you can walk through in five minutes: a BRD or requirements extract, a set of user stories with acceptance criteria, and a process map, with anything confidential removed. The free templates give you a starting point for each.

08How to prepare in a week

  1. Read the job description and list the five skills it stresses most. Prepare one example for each.
  2. Write STAR answers for the behavioural questions and the five scenarios above, and say them out loud.
  3. Prepare two portfolio pieces you can walk through: a BRD or set of user stories, and a process map.
  4. Learn the company's products and the regulations that apply to them.
  5. Do one mock interview with someone who'll give you honest feedback.

09Common questions

How do I answer BA interview questions with no BA experience?

Use examples from your current work, translated into BA language, and from a portfolio project. Say clearly which is which. Interviewers value honest, well-structured examples over inflated ones.

What should I bring to a business analyst interview?

A short portfolio you can walk through: a BRD or requirements extract, a set of user stories with acceptance criteria, and a process map. Remove any confidential details first.

How long should my interview answers be?

Around one to two minutes for most questions. For behavioural and scenario questions, use STAR and spend most of the time on your actions and the result.

Do business analyst interviews include a test?

Many do. Common tasks are writing user stories for a feature, mapping a short process, or reviewing a requirement and pointing out what is unclear or untestable.

What are the most common BA interview questions?

Tell me about yourself; what does a business analyst do; how do you write a BRD or a user story; how do you handle a stakeholder who keeps changing requirements; and how do you prioritise requirements. Prepare a short, specific answer for each.
